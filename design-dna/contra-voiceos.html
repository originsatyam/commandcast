01

The work is the colour. Everything else is a grey.

Contra is a professional network for independent creatives, and its landing page reads like a printed portfolio catalogue: a Swiss grid, a monochrome chrome, one acid wash, and type doing all of the branding.

The strategy is subtraction. Every interface element that would normally carry personality — section headers, filters, navigation, footers — is rendered in the same four greys. That leaves project imagery as the only saturated thing on screen. The system isn't "minimal" as a style; it's minimal as a business decision, because the product's inventory is other people's visual work.

The page still has a point of view, and it lives in three places: a lime wash that appears only behind promotional cards, an almost invisible 2px grid that makes projects tile like a contact sheet, and letter-spacing that increases at text sizes instead of tightening everything the way most modern systems do.

Vibe, in six words

  • Editorial — headlines read like a magazine masthead, not a SaaS pitch.
  • Monochrome — colour is content, never chrome.
  • Swiss — flush-left, generous outer margin, hairline rules.
  • Confident — no gradients, no glow, no persuasion effects.
  • Curated — "Projects we love" is a taste claim, not a sort order.
  • Dense where it counts — 4-up grids, but 96px of air between sections.

What it refuses

No accent colour in UI

There is exactly one chromatic UI token (a lime wash) and it is never used for text, links, focus, or status. The only other colour is photography.

No elevation drama

Two soft shadows exist, both on floating surfaces. Everything else separates with a 1px hairline or a #F2F4F7 tint.

No icon system as voice

Icons are 16px utility marks. Illustration, mascots, and emoji are absent — the work is the illustration.

Five signature moves

  1. 2px gutters. A 1351px content width carries four 336px tiles with 2px between them — near-seamless, like a contact sheet rather than a card grid.
  2. One acid wash. #F0FB96 appears as a flat promotional field. It never becomes a button, a badge, or a link.
  3. Inverted tracking. +0.02em at 19–23px, +0.1em at 12px caps, and only −0.01em at 58px. Most systems do the opposite.
  4. Full-pill actions only. 48px-tall buttons at 24px radius; 32px chips; 16px cards. Nothing in between.
  5. Section headers at 23px. The biggest recurring header on the page is smaller than most systems' body-large — the whitespace does the shouting.
Why this is hard to copy well: a monochrome system has nowhere to hide. Without colour, hierarchy depends entirely on type scale, tracking, and spacing discipline. Most imitations add a subtle gradient or a second accent within a week — and lose the whole effect.
02

Eight greys, one wash, and the work.

Sampled from computed styles, not from a brand PDF. Contrast is calculated on the final rendered pairs.

Core tokens

The colour logic

Three inks, two secondaries, three neutrals. That's the entire UI palette. The jump from #14171F to #677084 is large, so hierarchy reads almost binary — headline or supporting. The one mid-dark grey, #373F51, is reserved for interactive secondary text like “View more”, never for paragraphs, so the page never develops a third level of reading. That is why it feels quiet and slightly severe.

The lime is not an accent; it's a surface. It appears as a flat block behind promo content at #F0FB96, paired with ink. As a foreground on white it would be unreadable (roughly 1.1:1), so it never becomes a link or a badge. When you copy this system, copy that constraint — not just the hex.

The real palette is the work. The saturated colours on the page — lilac #DDD8FF, orchid #CD74DD, mint #D6E9EB, oat #EAE2D6, navy #242455 — are sampled from project thumbnails, not from UI tokens. They change with the inventory. That is the point of the monochrome chrome.

Copy with care: the muted grey sits at 4.51:1 on the surface tint. That passes AA by a hair. Change the tint by a few percent — a very common "make it warmer" tweak — and secondary copy drops below 4.5. If you need headroom, darken muted to #5F687A (≈5.4:1) and keep the tint.

03

Type is the brand, and it is rented.

Contra uses GT Standard by Grilli Type in two optical sizes: L for display, M for interface. It is a commercial licence, so the specimens below are rendered in the closest free stand-ins while keeping the original's measured size, weight, and tracking.

Primary family

GT Standard

Grilli Type · commercial, web licence separate. A geometric Swiss-modernist sans with a tall x-height, short descenders, open apertures, and single- or double-storey a/g alternates. The family is a system, not a single face: 7 weights × 6 widths × 3 optical sizes, 336 styles in total.

L / M are optical sizes — Large and Medium — not weights or widths. Contra sets display in L and 12–16px interface text in M, which is why the same page has both tight and slightly open spacing.

Why the tracking looks wrong

Most systems tighten as type grows. Contra also loosens as type shrinks: +0.46px at 23px, +0.38px at 19px, +1.2px at 12px caps, and only −0.58px at 58px.

That is an optical correction, not an accident: small type on a large canvas needs air to hold its colour, while display type needs its counters closed. The result reads as printed rather than app-like.

Do not copy the body leading blindly. Their 16px text runs at 18.4px (1.15). It works because 16px is almost always a label or a meta line. For paragraphs, use 1.45–1.6.

04

A catalogue grid with editorial margins.

Wide canvas, narrow text. The page uses 96px of vertical air to separate ideas, then packs projects into a near-seamless 2px grid.

The container is 1351px inside a 1440px viewport — roughly 36px outer margins at desktop, plus a fixed 74px navigation and a 69px sticky sub-navigation. Content is not centred in a narrow column; it runs almost edge to edge, then the type stays narrow (hero 452px, lede 344px).

The grid is the identity. Four columns of 336.33px with 2px gutters; three columns of 449.1px with 2px gutters. At that gutter width the images read as one field rather than separate cards — closer to a contact sheet than a marketplace. Cards still get 16px radii, but the spacing between them is almost nothing.

Measured layout

  • Viewport 1440 · content 1351 · outer 36
  • Nav fixed, 74px · sub-nav sticky 69px at top 72px
  • Project grid 4 × 336.33 @ 2px, or 3 × 449.1 @ 2px
  • Hero block 48px top, 52px bottom, 40px gap to content
  • Section rhythm 32–80px, usually 64
  • Footer 78px / 60px / 40px padding, 60px column gap
  • Density high in the grid, low in the prose

05

Shape is limited. Elevation is almost absent.

Three radii do 90% of the work, and elevation is limited to one ambient shadow plus a soft white halo. Depth is communicated with hairlines and tint, not blur.

Radii
Value Role
4px Micro badges, tiny indicators
10–12px Inline tags, small panels
16px Project and challenge cards
20px Floating promo panel
24px Buttons (48px tall = full pill)
32px Tag chips (37–40px tall)

Border-first, shadow-last. The hairline is #E5E7EB at roughly 1px — 1.24:1 against paper, which is deliberately below the 3:1 UI threshold because it separates, it doesn't inform. Functional boundaries (inputs, filters) get the same line but with a filled white surface behind them.

The shadow set is two recipes. An ambient pair — 0 4px 10px rgba(34,40,52,.05), −2px 18px 18px rgba(34,40,52,.04) — with the light coming from the left, used on floating cards; and a soft 24px white halo (0 0 24px rgba(228,232,247,.8)) on raised panels. Hover adds 3px of lift and the ambient pair.

No gradients in buttons or chrome. Gradients appear in project imagery and in edge-fade masks (white → transparent) on carousels. Buttons are flat fills; the dark button is #222834 with no border highlight.

07

170 milliseconds of acknowledgement.

Motion is a hover receipt, not a performance. Colours change fast; size and border changes get the slowest curve in the system.

Transition tokens by usage count
Property Duration Easing Used
background, border, color, opacity 170ms ease-out 35×
opacity (fades) 200ms ease-in-out 62×
all (generic) 320ms ease 62×
transform 200ms ease-out 22×
width, height, border 350ms ease 70×
fill (icons) 200ms ease-out 62×

No entrance animations on the landing page. There are no scroll reveals, no hero fade-ins, no parallax. The only keyframes in the CSS are decorative orb loops (5–8.5s) inside promotional artwork.

The 170ms standard is fast enough to feel like a state change rather than a transition. Hover lift is 3px, never more. If you're rebuilding the feel, this is the single most important number: 170ms, ease-out, colour and border only.

Layout transitions are slower — 320–350ms — because filter panels and expanding rows genuinely change size. Anything under 250ms there reads as a jump.

08

Curatorial, not promotional.

The copy behaves like a magazine editor wrote it: short nouns, no superlatives about the company, and taste presented as a service.

Headlines are claims about the work, not the platform. “Projects we love” is a curatorial statement; “Featured Projects” would be a CMS label. “Verified studios” is a standard; “Trusted Partners” would be a badge programme.

Sentence case everywhere, including buttons. No exclamation marks in product copy — only in community challenge tiles (“Join to submit!”). Numbers appear as social proof, unadorned: 9.2K, $10K, 3d left.

The subject is usually the creative, not Contra: “Get more creative”, “Find the right creative for your next project”, “Get paid commission-free”. The platform is the place, not the hero.

Lines worth studying

  • “Get more creative” — a benefit without a verb stack.
  • “The network for creative intelligence.” — category claim, one sentence.
  • “Projects we love” — taste as an editorial function.
  • “Verified studios” — trust without the word “trusted”.
  • “Post a job for free — creatives apply directly.” — the proposition, no funnel language.
  • “Let's go” — the only casual line in the system.
  • “Join to submit!” — exclamation reserved for community.

Rewrites

In their voice

“Get more creative.”
“Projects we love.”
“Verified studios.”
“Find the right creative for your next project.”

Off-brand

“Unlock your creative potential.”
“Featured Projects.”
“Trusted Partners.”
“Hire top-tier talent today!”

Rule of thumb: if a line could appear on any marketplace, it is not Contra. Replace the adjective with a standard or a curation claim.

Three moves to take

Kill the gutters, keep the radius

2px between tiles with 16px rounded corners gives you a portfolio field that still feels clickable. It only works if the imagery is consistent — which is a content-ops requirement, not a CSS one.

Loosen small type, tighten display

+0.02em at 19–23px and +0.1em at 12px caps makes a grotesque feel printed. Most systems only tighten, which is why they read as software.

Make trust a standard, not an adjective

“Verified studios”, a last-verified date, a source link. The copy move and the product move are the same move.

Two traps

8px labels and 1.15 body

The challenge tiles set prize labels at 8px/600, and 16px text at 1.15 leading. Both are fine for two-word metadata and wrong for anything a user must read. Do not let these numbers into a content surface.

A monochrome system with no content plan

Remove colour and every inconsistency shows. If your imagery is mixed-quality or your empty states are ugly, this system will expose it. The look is cheap; the editorial discipline is not.

01

The interface is a material, not a page.

VoiceOS is an AI voice assistant for Mac and Windows. Its landing page behaves like the operating system it lives in: a sky wallpaper, liquid glass, SF type, and exactly one blue that means “interactive”.

It sells a feel before it explains a feature. The first screen is a macOS-shaped window floating on a blue sky, with a headline at 76px and a glass download button. The product is a voice assistant; the design argument is that it belongs on your desktop, not in a browser tab.

The material does the persuading. Every raised surface is built from the same four-part recipe — a blurred backdrop, a white gradient overlay, an inset light from below, and a soft ambient drop — so cards, navigation, and buttons all feel like they were cut from the same pane of glass. Remove that recipe and the page becomes a generic SaaS landing with a blue sky.

Vibe, in six words

  • Native — window chrome, traffic lights, keyboard props.
  • Airy — 96–128px section rhythm, 576px text measure.
  • Tactile — every surface has a rim light and a shadow.
  • Precise — display type set solid at 60–72px.
  • Calm — one accent, no urgency, no countdowns.
  • Friendly — emoji, “Wall of Love”, “Press fn”.

What it refuses

No hard edges

Every container is rounded, from 5px app icons to 30px window corners. There is no square card, no hard divider, no full-bleed colour block with a 0 radius.

No stock photography of people

The imagery is the product and the sky. Social proof is text in glass cards, not smiling teams in an office.

No urgency mechanics

No countdowns, no red badges, no “only 2 seats left”. The strongest pressure is “7-day free trial · Cancel anytime”.

Five signature moves

  1. A notched glass navigation. The bar is a custom SVG silhouette with blur(3px) saturate(1.8), a white overlay, and a specular sheen — not a rounded rectangle with a backdrop filter.
  2. The four-part glass recipe. Blur + overlay gradient + inset light + ambient drop. Every raised element uses a variation of it.
  3. Optical tracking that scales. −2.28px at 76, −1.8px at 72, −1.5px at 60, −1.27px at 63.36, and 0 at 16. The bigger the type, the tighter it gets.
  4. Product props instead of illustrations. A physical fn keycap, macOS traffic lights, app icons at 5px radius, emoji inside H2s (👀, 🤝).
  5. Wide canvas, narrow voice. A 1280px container, a 576px measure, and centred sections with 96–128px above and below.
Why this is hard to copy: glass needs something behind it to refract. On a flat white page, the same recipe reads as a grey rounded box with a fuzzy border. The material is only half the system; the sky, the wallpaper, and the photography are the other half.
02

One blue, three skies, and a night section.

The neutrals are Tailwind's defaults. The palette is not a brand invention — it is a material palette, where light and atmosphere carry the identity.

Core tokens

The colour logic

The neutrals are not bespoke. Ink is Tailwind gray-900, muted is gray-500, the border is gray-200. What is bespoke is the atmosphere: a three-stop sky gradient (#DCEAFA → #EEF4FD → #FBFDFF) and the glass rim light. That's the actual design work — the greys are scaffolding.

The blue is a signal, not a text colour. Every occurrence of #0A84FF on the landing page is inside an SVG — strokes and fills on the product mark. No paragraph, link, or label uses it. Measured at 3.65:1 it would fail AA for body text anyway, so the restriction is both stylistic and correct.

The dark section is an inversion, not a theme. The privacy block flips to #030916 with white text, then returns to sky. There is no dark mode toggle and no dark token set on the landing page — don't mistake the section for a theming system.

Copy with care: muted #6B7280 measures 4.37:1 on the middle stop of the sky gradient — just under AA for 18px normal text. It passes on the lightest stop (4.74:1) and on white (4.83:1). If you rebuild this, either darken muted to #5F6878 or keep secondary copy on the lighter half of the gradient.

03

System type, set solid.

No webfont carries the page. It runs on the operating system's own face — SF Pro on Apple, Segoe UI on Windows — with DM Mono for labels and a serif that is loaded but never used.

Primary · system stack

SF Pro / system-ui

Not a webfont. The stack is -apple-system, BlinkMacSystemFont, "SF Pro Display", "SF Pro Text", system-ui. On a Mac you see SF; on Windows you see Segoe UI. Free to use because it belongs to the OS, but SF Pro itself is Apple-licensed and cannot be self-hosted for the web.

Label family · SIL OFL

DM Mono

Google Fonts, free. Used for eyebrow labels, the locale switch, footer text, and small technical captions at 12–18px, weights 400–600. Its presence is what stops the page from feeling entirely like an OS screenshot.

Accent serif · commercial

Recoleta

Latinotype, paid. The page declares @font-face for Recoleta-Regular.otf — but no element on the landing page renders in it. It is loaded and unused here; treat it as a brand asset reserved for other surfaces, not as part of this page's type system.

04

Wide canvas, narrow voice.

A 1280px stage, a 576px measure, and 96–128px of vertical air. The generosity is the hierarchy.

Containers are wide; paragraphs are not. The shell is max-width: 1280px with 32px gutters, giving a 1152px inner canvas. Headlines sit in 547–896px; lead copy caps around 768px; body copy around 576px. Nothing runs the full 1152.

Vertical rhythm is unusually large: 64px on compact feature sections, 96px on standard ones, 128px on the social-proof, FAQ, and closing sections. The hero alone is 900px tall at 1440 — the first screen contains one idea.

05

Glass is a recipe, not a colour.

Four layers produce every raised surface: a blurred backdrop, a white gradient overlay, an inset light, and an ambient drop. The rim light is the tell.

The recipe. A translucent base (white at 20–35%, or a 24%-white gradient), backdrop-filter: blur(3px) saturate(1.8) on navigation, inset 0 3px 3px rgba(0,0,0,.04) at the top, inset 0 -3px 3px rgba(255,255,255,.7) at the bottom, an ambient 0 10px 24px -4px rgba(0,0,0,.06), and a blue-tinted rim inset 0 0 3px 6px rgba(210,235,255,.35).

Radius is semantic. 30px for the outer window frame, 24px for cards, 18px for the app window, 12px for FAQ rows, 5px for app icons, 999px for every pill. There is no uniform radius; the size of the object determines the curve.

Light comes from below. The inset highlight sits on the bottom edge, not the top. That is why the glass reads as a physical slab catching a desk lamp rather than a bevelled button.

08

The product is the subject. You are the hand.

Short declaratives, second person, present tense. Privacy is stated as a fact, not a policy link. The warmth comes from props and punctuation, not exclamation marks.

Headlines are instructions or reframes. “Point anywhere on your screen.” “Press fn and start speaking.” “Stop looking 👀 for that file.” Each one describes an action the body already knows, which is why the page feels native rather than explanatory.

Emoji functions as punctuation — one per headline, placed where a comma would go, never as a bullet or a status. It's the main source of warmth in an otherwise Apple-cold layout.

Trust is a plain sentence. “Your audio is never stored on our servers and never used to train models.” No “bank-grade”, no shield icon, no policy link in the middle of the claim. The confidence is the design.

Claims are quantified, then colloquialised. A 1× / 4× / 10× productivity comparison does the arguing, and the social proof is a single phrase — “20,000+ voicepilled” — rather than a logo wall. The register stays human even when the evidence is a number.

Three moves to take

Set display type solid

Line-height 1.0 at 48–72px, 1.25 only when the headline wraps. It's the fastest way to make a web page feel like an app title — and it only works above ~40px.

Light from below

inset 0 -3px 3px rgba(255,255,255,.7) plus a blue-tinted rim. The bottom highlight is what separates real glass from a translucent div.

Use a prop, not an illustration

A keycap, a window bar, an app icon at 5px. One physical object per section beats a custom illustration system — and it renders in CSS.

Two traps

Glass on a flat page

Without a sky, a photograph, or a gradient behind it, the recipe collapses into a grey box with a fuzzy edge. The material needs a world to refract.

System type as a brand

SF Pro on a Mac looks inevitable; Segoe UI on Windows looks like a different product. If the brand must survive both, choose a licensed or OFL face and accept the loss of native feel.

01

Two answers to the same question.

Both pages are selling trust to a creative or technical audience. One does it by removing colour; the other by manufacturing light.

Contra

“The work is the colour.”

A catalogue. The chrome is monochrome so the inventory can be loud. Type is the brand: a licensed geometric sans, optical sizes, and tracking that loosens as it shrinks. Motion is a 170ms receipt.

Best at: marketplaces, portfolios, directories, editorial commerce.

VoiceOS

“The interface is a material.”

A native object. The page is built from sky, glass, and SF type, so the product feels installed before it is downloaded. Type is the OS; the material is the brand. Motion is atmosphere plus a 350ms settle.

Best at: consumer AI, desktop software, developer tools with an OS feel.

The common ground: neither system uses colour to create hierarchy. Contra uses value (four greys); VoiceOS uses light (rim, blur, gradient). Both are single-accent systems where the accent is almost decorative — which is why both feel expensive and slightly cold.

04

What to take, and from where.

A short routing table. Take mechanisms, not skins.

Marketplace / portfolio

Contra's grid + monochrome

2px gutters, four-up tiles, four greys, one wash. Only if your inventory is visually consistent — otherwise the grid exposes the mismatch.

Consumer AI / desktop app

VoiceOS's material + system type

A sky behind the glass, SF (or Inter) at display sizes, line-height 1.0, one blue signal. Gate the ambient motion on reduced-motion.

Editorial commerce

Contra's tracking inversion

Loosen text sizes (+0.02em) and tighten display (−0.01em). It makes a grotesque read as print without changing the family.

Trust-heavy product

Contra's standards language

“Verified studios”, a last-verified date, a source link. Name the standard instead of claiming trustworthiness.

Native feel on the web

VoiceOS's props

Keycap, window bar, app icon at 5px. One physical object per section, built in CSS, beats an illustration library.

Do not mix

Glass + 2px grid

The two systems fight: glass needs air and a background, the contact-sheet grid needs density and flatness. Pick the world before you pick the components.

The honest summary: Contra's system is mostly editorial discipline — cheap to copy, hard to sustain. VoiceOS's system is mostly material craft — expensive to copy, easy to overdo. Neither is a component library. Both are a set of restraints, and the restraints are the design.
