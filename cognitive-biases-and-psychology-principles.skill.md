# Cognitive Biases & Psychology Principles

## Purpose

Use this reference to identify relevant psychological patterns and UX principles when analyzing a situation or designing an intervention. Select only the principles that materially apply; do not force-fit the list to every problem.

## How to use this skill

1. **Clarify the goal and context.** Identify the audience, decision, environment, constraints, and desired outcome.
2. **Select the few relevant principles.** Prefer the smallest set that explains the situation. Avoid name-dropping many effects without showing how they apply.
3. **Explain the mechanism.** Describe what the principle suggests about attention, interpretation, motivation, choice, or memory.
4. **Translate into action.** Give a concrete, ethical design or communication change.
5. **Check trade-offs and evidence.** Distinguish a useful hypothesis from a proven causal claim. Test important assumptions with user research or experiments.
6. **Avoid manipulation.** Do not use these principles to deceive users, conceal material information, exploit vulnerable people, or manufacture false urgency or social proof.

## Source and scope

This catalogue follows the organization and short descriptions of Growth.Design's “106 Cognitive Biases & Principles That Affect Your UX” page:

- Source: https://growth.design/psychology
- The source organizes the catalogue into four groups: Information, Meaning, Time, and Memory.
- The source combines cognitive biases, psychological effects, heuristics, behavioral principles, and UX design concepts. They are **not all cognitive biases in the strict scientific sense**.
- The short descriptions below are source-style summaries, not full scientific definitions or evidence reviews.
- If a source label or definition seems simplified, retain it when reporting what the source says; separately flag any scientific caveat rather than silently rewriting the source.

## Selection framework

For each case, ask:

- **Attention:** What will people notice or ignore?
- **Interpretation:** What meaning or assumptions will they attach to it?
- **Choice and effort:** What makes an action easier, harder, more attractive, or more aversive?
- **Memory:** What is likely to be retained or recalled later?
- **Ethics:** Does the intervention support informed choice and user welfare?
- **Validation:** What observable result would confirm or disconfirm the hypothesis?

## Catalogue

### 1. Information — 29 principles

*How people filter, notice, and process information.*

1. **Hick's Law** — More options can make decisions harder.
2. **Confirmation Bias** — People look for evidence that confirms what they think.
3. **Priming** — Previous stimuli influence users' decisions.
4. **Cognitive Load** — The total mental effort required to complete a task.
5. **Anchoring Bias** — Users rely heavily on the first piece of information they see.
6. **Nudge** — Subtle hints can affect users' decisions.
7. **Progressive Disclosure** — Users are less overwhelmed when complex features are introduced later.
8. **Fitts's Law** — Large and nearby elements are easier to interact with.
9. **Banner Blindness** — Users tune out material they are repeatedly exposed to.
10. **Decoy Effect** — An additional option can make another option easier to choose by comparison.
11. **Framing** — The way information is presented affects how users make decisions.
12. **Attentional Bias** — People's thoughts and concerns filter what they pay attention to.
13. **Empathy Gap** — People underestimate how much emotions influence behavior.
14. **Visual Anchors** — Elements used to guide users' eyes.
15. **Von Restorff Effect** — People tend to notice items that stand out.
16. **Visual Hierarchy** — The order in which people perceive visual information.
17. **Selective Attention** — People filter out parts of their environment when focused.
18. **Survivorship Bias** — People neglect cases that did not survive a selection process.
19. **Juxtaposition** — Nearby and similar elements may be perceived as a single unit.
20. **Signifiers** — Elements that communicate what they will do.
21. **Contrast** — Attention is drawn to elements with greater visual weight or difference.
22. **External Trigger** — A prompt itself contains information about what to do next.
23. **Centre-Stage Effect** — People may prefer the middle option in a set.
24. **Law of Proximity** — Nearby elements are often perceived as related.
25. **Tesler's Law** — Simplifying a system can transfer some complexity to its users or elsewhere in the system.
26. **Spark Effect** — Users may be more likely to act when the required effort is small.
27. **Feedback Loop** — Feedback communicates what happened after a user takes action.
28. **Expectations Bias** — People tend to be influenced by their expectations.
29. **Aesthetic-Usability Effect** — People may perceive aesthetically pleasing designs as easier to use.

### 2. Meaning — 32 principles

*How people interpret information, form assumptions, and understand what they encounter.*

30. **Social Proof** — Users adapt their behavior based on what others do.
31. **Scarcity** — People may value things more when they are in limited supply.
32. **Curiosity Gap** — People seek out missing information.
33. **Mental Model** — Users have preconceived ideas about how things work.
34. **Familiarity Bias** — People tend to prefer familiar experiences.
35. **Skeuomorphism** — Users may adapt more easily to things that resemble real-world objects.
36. **Reciprocity** — People feel a need to reciprocate when they receive something.
37. **Singularity Effect** — People may care disproportionately about an individual compared with a group.
38. **Variable Reward** — Unexpected rewards can be especially engaging.
39. **Aha! Moment** — The point when new users first realize a product's value.
40. **Goal Gradient Effect** — Motivation can increase as people get closer to a goal.
41. **Occam's Razor** — Simpler explanations are often preferable when they explain the evidence adequately.
42. **Noble Edge Effect** — Users may prefer socially responsible companies.
43. **Hawthorne Effect** — People may change their behavior when they know they are being observed.
44. **Halo Effect** — Overall impressions can influence judgments about particular traits.
45. **Miller's Law** — The source describes working memory as holding roughly 7 ± 2 items; treat this as a simplified historical rule of thumb, not a universal capacity limit.
46. **Unit Bias** — One unit of something can feel like the optimal amount.
47. **Flow State** — A state of deep immersion and focus on a task.
48. **Authority Bias** — People may give greater weight to an authority figure's opinion.
49. **Pseudo-Set Framing** — Tasks presented as part of a set can feel more tempting to complete.
50. **Group Attractiveness Effect** — Individual items may seem more attractive when presented in a group.
51. **Curse of Knowledge** — People may fail to recognize that others have less knowledge than they do.
52. **Self-Initiated Triggers** — People may be more likely to interact with prompts they set up themselves.
53. **Survey Bias** — Survey responses can be skewed toward socially acceptable answers.
54. **Cognitive Dissonance** — Holding conflicting ideas can be psychologically uncomfortable.
55. **Feedforward** — Users know what to expect before taking action.
56. **Hindsight Bias** — After an outcome, people may overestimate how well they could have predicted it.
57. **Law of Similarity** — Elements that look similar are often perceived as related.
58. **Law of Prägnanz** — People tend to interpret ambiguous visual input in simpler, more organized forms.
59. **Streisand Effect** — Attempts to suppress information can increase attention to it.
60. **Spotlight Effect** — People tend to overestimate how much others notice them.
61. **Fresh Start Effect** — A sense of a new beginning can encourage action.

### 3. Time — 28 principles

*How people make decisions, respond to effort, and use mental shortcuts.*

62. **Labor Illusion** — People may value something more when they see the work behind it.
63. **Default Bias** — People often stick with an established default.
64. **Investment Loops** — When users invest effort or resources, they may be more likely to return.
65. **Loss Aversion** — Losses can weigh more heavily than equivalent gains.
66. **Commitment & Consistency** — People tend to act consistently with previous actions or commitments.
67. **Sunk Cost Effect** — People may be reluctant to abandon something they have invested in.
68. **Reactance** — People may resist a behavior when they feel forced.
69. **Law of the Instrument** — People may over-rely on familiar tools or approaches.
70. **Temptation Bundling** — A difficult task may feel more appealing when paired with something desired.
71. **Dunning–Kruger Effect** — The source describes people as potentially overestimating their skills when they know little; avoid using this label as a casual diagnosis of individuals.
72. **Discoverability** — How easily users can find features.
73. **Second-Order Effect** — The consequences of the consequences of an action.
74. **Decision Fatigue** — Repeated decisions may make later decisions harder; the size and generality of this effect depend on context.
75. **Observer-Expectancy Effect** — Researchers' expectations can influence participants or observations.
76. **Weber's Law** — Detecting a difference often depends on the relative size of a change, not just its absolute size.
77. **Parkinson's Law** — Work can expand to fill the time available for its completion.
78. **Affect Heuristic** — Current feelings can influence judgment.
79. **Hyperbolic Discounting** — People may prioritize immediate rewards over larger future rewards.
80. **Chronoception** — People's perception of time is subjective.
81. **Cashless Effect** — Spending may feel different when payment is less tangible.
82. **Self-Serving Bias** — People may take credit for positive outcomes and attribute negative outcomes externally.
83. **Pareto Principle** — Roughly 80% of effects may come from 20% of causes in some contexts; this is a heuristic, not a universal law.
84. **Backfire Effect** — The source describes challenged convictions as sometimes becoming stronger; do not assume this response is inevitable or common in every context.
85. **False Consensus Effect** — People may overestimate how much others agree with them.
86. **Bandwagon Effect** — People may adopt beliefs or behaviors because many others have done so.
87. **Barnum–Forer Effect** — People may see vague, general personality descriptions as uniquely applicable to themselves.
88. **IKEA Effect** — People may value things more when they have helped create them.
89. **Planning Fallacy** — People tend to underestimate how long tasks will take.

### 4. Memory — 17 principles

*How people remember experiences, information, and interactions.*

90. **Provide Exit Points** — Invite users to leave an app at an appropriate moment.
91. **Peak-End Rule** — People may judge an experience largely by its peak and its ending.
92. **Sensory Appeal** — Users may engage more with experiences that appeal to multiple senses.
93. **Zeigarnik Effect** — In some contexts, incomplete tasks can be more memorable than completed ones.
94. **Endowment Effect** — People may value something more when they feel it is theirs.
95. **Chunking** — Grouped information can be easier to remember.
96. **Delighters** — Unexpected, enjoyable details can make an experience memorable.
97. **Internal Trigger** — A memory or internal state prompts a user to take action.
98. **Picture Superiority Effect** — People often remember pictures better than words alone.
99. **Method of Loci** — Associating information with locations can aid memory.
100. **Shaping** — Gradually reinforcing actions that approach a target behavior.
101. **Recognition Over Recall** — Recognizing an option is often easier than retrieving it from memory unaided.
102. **Storytelling Effect** — Stories can make information easier to remember than isolated facts.
103. **Negativity Bias** — Negative events may receive more attention or be remembered more strongly than positive ones.
104. **Availability Heuristic** — People judge likelihood or frequency partly by how easily examples come to mind.
105. **Spacing Effect** — Learning is often improved when study sessions are spaced over time.
106. **Serial Position Effect** — People often recall the first and last items in a sequence more readily than middle items.

## Output format for analyses using this skill

When applying the catalogue to a real problem, use this structure:

1. **Situation:** Briefly describe the decision or behavior being analyzed.
2. **Relevant principles:** Select approximately 3–5 principles that genuinely fit; use fewer when sufficient.
3. **Mechanism:** Explain how each principle might influence behavior.
4. **Practical application:** Recommend a concrete change or experiment.
5. **Risks and ethics:** Identify manipulation risks, accessibility concerns, and possible unintended effects.
6. **Validation:** State what evidence or metric would support or reject the hypothesis.

Do not claim that a principle caused an outcome merely because the situation resembles its label. Treat the catalogue as a hypothesis generator and validate consequential decisions with evidence.
